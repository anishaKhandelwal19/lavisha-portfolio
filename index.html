Brand Strategy • Aug 2026 • Notes on Trent, Westside & Zudio

A brand is defined as much by what it chooses not to do as what it chooses to do.

A strong brand is not only defined by what it does. It is also defined by what it deliberately chooses not to do.

That was one of my biggest takeaways from listening to Venkatesalu P, CEO & MD of Trent Ltd., on the Indian Business Podcast.

As a marketing student, I found Trent’s approach to building Westside and Zudio particularly interesting — clear positioning, disciplined store expansion, private-label strength, and a strong willingness to stay consistent with what the brands stand for.

But it also got me thinking about one important question:

When should a brand stop protecting its choices and start evolving them?

Zudio: Is the value proposition changing?

I understand why Zudio has largely stayed focused on its offline model. It keeps the business relatively simple and helps control costs.

But from a consumer’s perspective, I wonder if the value equation is becoming less clear.

Some categories, especially footwear, can feel repetitive, while prices have increased over time. At the same time, the perceived improvement in quality doesn't always feel proportional.

When a pair of jeans is around ₹1,200, my comparison set changes. I'm no longer looking only at local value retailers — I'm also looking at options from H&M, Myntra and other fashion brands.

And that leads to a very important brand question:

“Why Zudio?”

Perhaps Zudio's next phase could focus on:

  • More frequent collection refreshes
  • Even sharper and clearer price points
  • Stronger differentiation in quality as prices move upward
  • Creating more reasons for customers to return frequently

Westside: Where technology could add another layer

Westside is a different story.

With its broader assortment, complete-look approach and experience-led stores, I think technology could enhance what the brand already does well.

Imagine trying on a top, scanning it through a smart screen or the app, and immediately receiving recommendations for jeans, footwear, accessories or a complete outfit.

Take it one step further — imagine seeing a virtual preview of the entire look before buying.

That could connect the offline and digital shopping experience, make styling easier, and potentially increase basket size at the same time.

The bigger lesson

What I took away from the podcast wasn't simply that Trent has built successful retail brands.

It was that consistency is powerful — but consistency cannot mean standing still.

The choices that create a brand's identity today may need to evolve when consumer expectations, competition and the market change.

For me, that's one of the most interesting challenges in brand management:

Knowing which choices to protect — and knowing when it's time to rethink them.

What would you change about the customer experience at Zudio or Westside?

Brand Experience • Aug 2026 • Notes on Beanly Coffee

What if your best-selling SKU became your biggest inventory risk?

A great product story only works when customers actually discover it.

I recently watched Khul Ke Scale, featuring Shantanu Deshpande in conversation with Rahul Jain and Samayesh Khanna, Co-founders of Beanly Coffee, along with Shiv Shivakumar and Toshan Tamhane.

The discussion around building a modern consumer brand got me curious about something beyond the conversation itself:

What does that brand experience look like from a customer's perspective?

So, I visited Beanly Coffee's website as a first-time customer — and a few things caught my attention.

1. The homepage could tell the bigger Beanly story

Beanly isn't just one coffee product anymore.

Its portfolio spans instant coffee, pour-over bags, nitro cold brew, merchandise and more.

Yet, the first thing a new visitor sees is heavily centred around a single product.

For an existing customer, that may not matter much. But for a first-time visitor, the homepage is an opportunity to communicate:

“Here's everything Beanly can offer you.”

Making the broader ecosystem more visible could potentially create stronger curiosity and encourage exploration.

2. Quick commerce can be a discovery engine

Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart are no longer just places customers go when they need something immediately.

They are increasingly becoming discovery platforms.

This creates an interesting opportunity for brands like Beanly.

What if customers could discover smaller trial packs, breakfast combinations or personalised add-ons while shopping?

Even a small ₹9 trial pack could lower the barrier for someone who has never tried the brand.

Today's trial could become tomorrow's repeat purchase.

3. Reward customers with more than discounts

Loyalty doesn't always have to mean another percentage-off coupon.

For a coffee brand, the reward itself can become part of the experience.

For example:

  • A complimentary Beanly mug above a certain order value
  • Buy 4, Get 1 Free offers
  • Curated coffee bundles for gifting
  • Limited-edition merchandise for repeat customers

These ideas could potentially increase basket size while giving customers a more tangible reason to come back.

My biggest takeaway?

Innovation doesn't stop at creating the product.

The next challenge is making sure customers notice it, understand it, try it and eventually come back for it.

What I found interesting about this episode was how it made me look at Beanly not just as a coffee brand, but through the lens of the entire customer journey.

These are simply observations from a first-time customer's perspective after listening to the conversation and exploring the brand.

I'd love to know — if you visited Beanly for the first time, what would you change or improve?

Retail Strategy • Aug 2026 • Notes on Manyavar

The Manyavar Playbook: How Retail Becomes a Brand

What does it take to turn a traditional retail business into a powerful consumer brand?

I recently listened to a podcast featuring Vedant Modi, and the Manyavar journey stood out to me as a fascinating combination of consumer understanding, data, retail strategy and brand building.

A few ideas particularly stayed with me:

1. Start with the consumer, not assumptions

One of the most important lessons is surprisingly simple:

Don't assume you know your customer before they even walk into the store.

Understanding what customers actually want — their preferences, tastes and behaviours — can become the foundation for everything from product decisions to store strategy.

2. Find the gap before building the brand

Indian ethnic wear was traditionally dominated by tailors, customised clothing and fragmented retail.

Manyavar saw an opportunity to make Indian occasion wear more organized, accessible and ready-to-wear.

The interesting part wasn't just selling ethnic clothing. It was making the buying experience easier and more scalable.

3. Let data influence the decisions

From colours, fabrics and embroidery to regional preferences and weather patterns, data can influence much more than just marketing.

It can shape:

  • Product design
  • Demand forecasting
  • Inventory planning
  • Store assortment
  • Replenishment

This is where retail becomes much more than simply putting products on shelves.

4. The store experience matters

Manyavar's approach to customers being treated as guests highlights something important about Indian retail.

Product alone may bring someone into a store, but service, cultural understanding and employee training can determine how they remember the brand.

5. Sometimes growth requires thinking beyond margins

One lesson that stood out was the importance of working-capital management.

There can be situations where selling inventory at a lower margin — or even at a loss — makes sense if it improves cash flow and helps the overall business remain healthy.

It was a good reminder that profitability isn't always about maximizing the margin on every individual sale.

6. Make celebrity marketing feel real

Celebrity endorsements can create awareness, but the stronger strategy is when the celebrity connects naturally with a real consumer insight or cultural moment.

The objective isn't simply:

“A celebrity is wearing our product.”

It is:

“This is a moment our customer genuinely relates to.”

7. Build a retail model that can scale

The podcast also made me think about the different franchise structures:

  • COCO — Company Owned, Company Operated
  • COFO — Company Owned, Franchise Operated
  • FOFO — Franchise Owned, Franchise Operated
  • FOCO — Franchise Owned, Company Operated

What I found particularly interesting is Manyavar's approach, which sits between COFO and FOFO, where the company generally takes the lease while the franchise partner operates the store.

That structure can provide greater control over the retail infrastructure while still benefiting from the local knowledge and entrepreneurial mindset of franchise partners.

And that matters in India because consumer preferences can change dramatically across regions.

But here's the bigger question...

Manyavar didn't just build a business around ethnic clothing.

It built a strong association with celebrations, weddings and occasions.

And that made me wonder:

Is Manyavar's biggest competitive advantage really its products — or is it owning the "celebration wear" occasion in the consumer's mind?

For me, that's perhaps the most interesting part of the Manyavar playbook.

Because the strongest brands don't just sell products.

They own a moment in the customer's life.
